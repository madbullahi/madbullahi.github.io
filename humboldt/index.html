Humboldt project · Goethe University Frankfurt

Removing pathogens
from reclaimed water with
a living, low carbon filter

A research project testing how far a treatment step built on Daphnia can remove bacterial and viral pathogens, as well as chemicals, from wastewater. For utilities and researchers who need water reuse to stay affordable.

Start a collaboration

Funded by the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation. The underlying technology won a £100,000 Ofwat Water Discovery Challenge award in 2026.

Overview

Water reuse needs treatment that poorer regions can afford

The problem

Reused water can still carry what makes people ill

Treated wastewater can keep traces of pharmaceuticals and industrial chemicals, along with bacteria and viruses, including bacteria that carry antimicrobial resistance. Advanced treatment that removes them often costs too much, or uses too much energy, for the regions that depend on water reuse most.

The approach

Let filter feeders do the work

Daphnia, the water flea, feeds by filtering the water around it. Added as a final stage after conventional treatment, it takes up and breaks down contaminants. In a four week mesocosm trial with real wastewater it removed pharmaceuticals. This project measures how well the same step removes pathogens.

Benefits

What this treatment step offers

  • Low cost and low carbon

    Living organisms do the filtering, so the step needs little energy, no added chemicals, and leaves no harmful byproducts.

  • Tested in real wastewater

    A four week mesocosm trial removed pharmaceuticals from real wastewater, published in Science of the Total Environment in 2023.

  • Built on 300+ experiments

    More than 300 controlled exposure experiments across five classes of chemical pollutant sit behind the method.

  • Focused on pathogens and resistance

    The project tracks bacterial and viral pathogens and antimicrobial resistance, not chemicals alone.

  • Designed for places short of water

    Kept low cost and low tech, so it can serve developing economies where water is scarce and sanitation is limited.

Every water flea is a tiny filter. Put millions to work, and wastewater comes out cleaner.

How it works

Three stages, one living filter

  1. Treat as usual

    Wastewater first passes through the plant's standard primary and secondary treatment.

  2. Add a Daphnia stage

    In a final tank, Daphnia filter the water as they feed, taking up chemicals and microbes.

  3. Measure what is removed

    Chemical analysis, microbiome profiling and pathogen assays show what the Daphnia remove and what still passes through.

Questions

Questions people ask about the project

What is the aim of the project?

To find out how well a treatment step built on Daphnia removes bacterial and viral pathogens from reclaimed water, alongside the chemicals it already removes, while keeping that step low in cost and carbon.

Who funds it, and where is it based?

The Alexander von Humboldt Foundation funds it through a Research Fellowship for Postdocs hosted at Goethe University Frankfurt. Muhammad is currently on a research visit at the University of Birmingham.

Why use Daphnia?

Daphnia are small freshwater crustaceans that feed by filtering the water around them. They are among the best studied animals in ecotoxicology, so their responses to pollutants are well understood.

Has the approach worked outside the lab?

Yes, for chemicals. A four week mesocosm trial in real wastewater removed pharmaceuticals, and the technology has since moved to commercial demonstration through Daphne Water Solutions.

Does it remove pathogens yet?

That is the question this project answers. Results will be shared through publications and on the project log as they come in.

Who should get in touch?

Water utilities and treatment plant operators, microbiologists and virologists, funders, and researchers working on water reuse or antimicrobial resistance.

What could a collaboration look like?

Joint funding applications, an exchange of samples or data, a pilot trial at a treatment site, or shared analysis of pathogen and resistance data.

Where can I follow progress?

On the project log and the publication list at madbullahi.github.io.
